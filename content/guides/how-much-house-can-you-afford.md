Lenders will tell you the most they will lend. That is not the same as what you can comfortably afford. This guide covers both numbers: how the bank arrives at its limit, and how to set your own.

## The 28/36 rule

The classic guideline has two parts:

- **Front-end ratio (28%)**: your total housing payment should not exceed 28% of gross monthly income. The housing payment means principal, interest, property tax, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance and any HOA dues, often abbreviated PITI.
- **Back-end ratio (36%)**: housing plus all other debt payments (car loans, student loans, credit card minimums) should not exceed 36% of gross income.

On a $90,000 salary, gross monthly income is $7,500. The 28% limit is a $2,100 housing payment; the 36% limit is $2,700 for all debts combined. With a $400 car payment and $200 in student loans, the housing payment would be capped at $2,100 by both tests.

Many lenders go beyond these ratios. Conventional loans often allow back-end ratios up to 43% or even 50% with strong credit and reserves, and FHA loans can stretch further. The 28/36 rule is conservative by design, which is why it remains a good personal benchmark even when a lender offers more.

## From payment to price

A monthly payment converts into a loan amount through the mortgage formula, and the loan plus your down payment gives the price. Using a $2,100 housing budget:

1. Set aside the non-loan costs. Property tax varies enormously by location but 1.1% of the home's value per year is a common national figure; insurance might run $150 a month. Call it $500 a month for taxes and insurance on a home around $400,000.
2. That leaves $1,600 for principal and interest.
3. At 6.5% over 30 years, $1,600 a month supports a loan of about $253,000.
4. With a $50,000 down payment, the price range tops out near $300,000.

Change the rate to 5.5% and the same $1,600 supports $282,000. Change it to 7.5% and it falls to $229,000. Rates move the affordable price far more than most buyers expect.

## Down payment and PMI

Putting down less than 20% on a conventional loan means paying private mortgage insurance, typically 0.3% to 1.5% of the loan per year until you reach 20% equity. On a $280,000 loan at 0.7%, PMI adds about $163 a month to the housing payment and therefore counts against the 28% test.

A larger down payment does three things at once: it lowers the loan, it may remove PMI, and it can earn a slightly better rate. The trade-off is time. Saving for years to reach 20% while rents and prices rise can cost more than a few years of PMI. Many buyers put down 5% to 10%, then request PMI removal once appreciation and payments get them to 20%.

## Costs the ratio ignores

The 28% test covers the mortgage payment. Owning a home costs more than that:

- **Maintenance.** Budget 1% to 2% of the home's value per year. On a $300,000 home that is $250 to $500 a month, every month, on average.
- **Utilities.** Often higher than in a rental, especially for older or larger homes.
- **Closing costs.** 2% to 5% of the price, paid up front. On $300,000 that is $6,000 to $15,000 beyond the down payment.
- **Moving and furnishing.** Rarely under a few thousand dollars.
- **Property tax increases.** Reassessment after a sale can raise the bill well above what the previous owner paid.

A comfortable budget leaves room for these after the mortgage is paid.

## Your own test: the leftover

Instead of a ratio, ask what remains after the full cost of the home. Take your monthly take-home pay (not gross), subtract the housing payment, maintenance reserve, utilities and all other fixed obligations, and look at what is left for food, transport, saving and life. If that number is thin, the house is too expensive regardless of what the lender approves. Because the 28% rule uses gross income, two households with the same salary but different tax situations or retirement contributions can end up with very different leftovers.

## Fixed versus adjustable rates

An adjustable-rate mortgage starts with a lower rate for 5, 7 or 10 years and then resets annually. The lower initial payment can make a larger loan "affordable" on paper. Treat it with care: qualify yourself on the payment at the fully adjusted rate, not the teaser.

## A worked example

Household income $120,000; take-home about $7,400 a month; a $450 car payment; $60,000 saved.

- 28% of $10,000 gross = $2,800 housing cap. 36% back-end = $3,600 less $450 = $3,150, so the 28% test binds.
- Reserve $600 for taxes and insurance; $2,200 remains for principal and interest.
- At 6.5% over 30 years, $2,200 supports about $348,000. Add a $50,000 down payment (keeping $10,000 for closing and reserves) for a price around $398,000.
- With 12.6% down, PMI of about $200 a month applies, pushing the true housing payment to roughly $3,000, above the 28% line.

A price nearer $360,000 brings the full payment back inside the guideline and leaves about $3,500 of take-home pay each month after housing, car and a $400 maintenance reserve. That is a comfortable purchase. The $398,000 version is approvable but tight, and one surprise repair away from stress.

The lender's number tells you the ceiling. Your own leftover tells you where to stand.
