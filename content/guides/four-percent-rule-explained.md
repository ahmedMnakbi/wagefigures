"How much do I need to retire?" has a surprisingly short answer: about 25 times what you spend in a year. That multiple comes from the 4% rule, the most widely used starting point in retirement planning. Here is what it says, where it came from, and how to adapt it.

## What the rule says

In the first year of retirement, withdraw 4% of your investment portfolio. Each year after that, increase the dollar amount by inflation, regardless of what the market does. Historically, a portfolio split roughly evenly between stocks and bonds has survived 30 years of such withdrawals in the large majority of U.S. market histories.

Turned around, 4% a year means your portfolio needs to be 1 ÷ 0.04 = 25 times your annual spending.

| Monthly spending | Annual | Portfolio at 4% |
|---|---|---|
| $3,000 | $36,000 | $900,000 |
| $4,000 | $48,000 | $1,200,000 |
| $5,000 | $60,000 | $1,500,000 |
| $7,000 | $84,000 | $2,100,000 |

## Where it comes from

The figure was popularised by financial planner William Bengen in 1994 and reinforced by the 1998 "Trinity study" from three professors at Trinity University. Both tested fixed withdrawal rates against every rolling 30-year period in U.S. market history going back to the 1920s. Rates above 4% failed in a meaningful share of periods, particularly those that began just before a severe downturn. Four percent was the highest rate that survived almost all of them.

The research assumed a U.S.-only portfolio, no fees, no taxes and a fixed 30-year horizon. Each of those assumptions is worth revisiting for your own plan.

## Only count what your savings must cover

The rule applies to spending your portfolio has to fund, not total spending. Subtract guaranteed income first:

- Monthly spending goal: $5,000
- Expected Social Security: $1,900
- Portfolio must supply: $3,100 a month, or $37,200 a year
- Portfolio needed at 4%: $930,000

A pension, rental income or part-time work reduces the target the same way. This is why two people with the same lifestyle can need very different savings.

## When to use a lower rate

A 4% starting rate is a reasonable default for a 30-year retirement. Consider 3% to 3.5% when:

- You are retiring early and may need the money for 40 years or more.
- Your portfolio is mostly bonds or cash, which have lower expected returns.
- You want to leave a sizeable inheritance.
- Fees on your investments exceed about 0.5% a year.

At 3.5% the multiple is 28.6; at 3% it is 33.3. For $48,000 a year that means $1.37 million or $1.6 million instead of $1.2 million. The difference is large, which is why the choice of rate deserves more attention than most people give it.

## When a higher rate can work

Rigidly increasing withdrawals with inflation every year is the harshest version of the rule. Retirees who can trim spending after a bad market year, who have flexible expenses, or who are willing to spend more if returns are strong can often start at 4.5% to 5% without greater risk. Guardrail strategies formalise this: cut the withdrawal by 10% after a poor year, raise it after a strong one.

## Turning the target into a monthly saving

Suppose you need $1.2 million in 25 years and expect a 7% average annual return. The monthly saving required is about $1,480. Over 30 years it falls to roughly $985 a month; over 20 years it rises to about $2,300. Time does most of the work: in the 30-year case you contribute about $355,000 and growth provides the other $845,000.

If 7% feels optimistic, run the numbers at 5% as well. The required saving over 30 years rises to about $1,440 a month. Planning with the lower figure and treating higher returns as a bonus is safer than the reverse.

## Taxes

Withdrawals from traditional 401(k) and IRA accounts are taxed as income. If your money is mostly pre-tax, your portfolio needs to supply your spending plus the tax on it. Someone paying an effective 15% rate who wants $48,000 to spend needs to withdraw about $56,500, which at 4% means a portfolio of about $1.41 million rather than $1.2 million. Roth accounts and taxable accounts (where only gains are taxed) soften this.

## A checklist

1. Estimate annual spending in retirement in today's dollars.
2. Subtract Social Security, pensions and any other guaranteed income.
3. Gross up for taxes if most savings are pre-tax.
4. Multiply by 25 for a 30-year horizon, or by 29–33 for a longer one.
5. Work backwards to a monthly saving using a conservative return.

The 4% rule is a planning tool, not a guarantee. Revisit the numbers every few years, and especially in the five years before and after retiring, when the sequence of returns matters most.
