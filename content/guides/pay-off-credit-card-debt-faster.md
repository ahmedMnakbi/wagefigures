Credit card debt is expensive in a way few other debts are: rates of 20% to 30% are normal, and interest is charged on the balance every single day. The good news is that the same arithmetic that makes it costly also makes paying it off unusually rewarding. Here is how to do it with the least interest and the least frustration.

## Why the balance barely moves

Take $6,000 at 24% APR. The monthly interest rate is 2%, so the first month's interest is $120. A $150 payment reduces the balance by only $30. At that pace the card takes nearly nine years to clear and costs more than $9,000 in interest.

Minimum payments are designed around this. A typical minimum is 1% of the balance plus interest, so on $6,000 at 24% it starts around $180 and shrinks as the balance falls. Because the payment keeps shrinking, the payoff stretches over many years. Paying a **fixed** amount every month, rather than the minimum, is the single most important change.

## Fix the payment, then raise it

Decide on a monthly amount you will pay regardless of what the statement says. The effect of raising it is dramatic because every extra dollar goes straight to principal:

| Payment on $6,000 at 24% | Payoff time | Total interest |
|---|---|---|
| $150 | 8 years 9 months | $9,700 |
| $200 | 3 years 7 months | $2,580 |
| $300 | 2 years 1 month | $1,420 |
| $500 | 1 year 2 months | $740 |

Going from $150 to $200 a month, a difference of $50, saves over $7,000. That is the leverage of attacking a high-rate balance.

## Choosing an order: avalanche or snowball

With several cards, pay the minimum on all of them and send every extra dollar to one card at a time.

**Avalanche**: target the highest APR first. This minimises total interest and is mathematically best.

**Snowball**: target the smallest balance first. You clear a card sooner, which many people find motivating, and the freed-up minimum rolls into the next card. It costs a little more in interest.

The difference in total cost is usually modest. If you have struggled to stick with a plan before, the snowball's early win can be worth its small extra cost. If you are disciplined and the rates differ a lot, the avalanche saves more. Either beats indecision.

## Balance transfers

A balance transfer card charges 0% interest for an introductory period, commonly 12 to 21 months, in exchange for a fee of 3% to 5% of the amount moved. Moving $6,000 for a 3% fee costs $180 up front. Pay $400 a month and the balance is gone in about 16 months with no further interest, versus roughly $1,000 of interest on the original card over the same period.

Things to check:

- **The fee** is added to the balance. Include it when planning the payoff.
- **The promo end date.** Any balance left reverts to a normal rate, often 25% or more.
- **New purchases** may not get the 0% rate. Use a different card for spending.
- **Approval** typically requires good credit, and the limit may be lower than the balance you want to move.
- **One late payment** can end the promotion early on some cards.

A transfer only works if you keep paying aggressively. Treat the 0% period as a deadline, not a holiday.

## Consolidation loans

A personal loan at, say, 11% to replace cards at 24% cuts the rate and converts revolving debt into a fixed schedule with a clear end date. On $6,000 over three years at 11%, the payment is $196 and total interest about $1,070, roughly a third of what the card would charge at a similar payment. Loans suit people who want one payment and a fixed finish line, and who will not run the cards back up. Watch for origination fees of 1% to 8%, which should be included in the comparison.

## Negotiating the rate

Calling the issuer and asking for a lower APR works more often than people expect, especially with a good payment history or a competing offer in hand. Even a few points helps: on $6,000 at $200 a month, moving from 24% to 18% saves about $600 and four months.

## Stop the balance growing

None of this works if new charges keep landing on the card. While paying down a balance:

- Switch everyday spending to a debit card or cash.
- Build a small emergency buffer, even $500, so the next surprise does not go on the card.
- Set the fixed payment as an automatic transfer the day after payday.

## When to get help

If minimum payments across all cards exceed what you can pay, a non-profit credit counselling agency can set up a debt management plan with reduced rates. Be wary of for-profit "debt settlement" firms that charge large fees and advise you to stop paying, which damages your credit and racks up penalties.

## The payoff

A $6,000 balance cleared in two years instead of nine does more than save $8,000 in interest. It frees $300 a month that, invested at 7%, grows to about $150,000 over the next 20 years. High-rate debt is the first problem to solve because everything else in a financial plan gets easier once it is gone.
