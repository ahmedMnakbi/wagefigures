Compound interest is interest on interest. The idea is simple; its consequences are not intuitive, because human beings expect things to grow in straight lines and compounding produces curves. The best way to build intuition is to look at actual numbers.

## Simple versus compound

Put $10,000 in an account paying 6% a year.

With **simple** interest you earn $600 every year, forever. After 30 years you have $10,000 + 30 × $600 = $28,000.

With **compound** interest the first year also earns $600, but the second year earns 6% of $10,600, which is $636. The third year earns 6% of $11,236. After 30 years you have about $57,435 with annual compounding, or $60,226 if interest is added monthly. Compounding more than doubled the result of simple interest, and the gap keeps widening.

## The curve

Here is $10,000 at 7% a year, compounded monthly:

| Years | Balance | Gain in that decade |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | $10,000 | — |
| 10 | $20,097 | $10,097 |
| 20 | $40,387 | $20,290 |
| 30 | $81,165 | $40,778 |
| 40 | $163,115 | $81,950 |

Each decade adds about twice as much as the one before. The final ten years contribute more than the first thirty combined. This is the whole argument for starting early: the money you invest at 25 does its heaviest lifting in your fifties and sixties.

## The rule of 72

Divide 72 by the annual rate to get the approximate number of years for money to double:

- 3% → about 24 years
- 6% → about 12 years
- 8% → about 9 years
- 12% → about 6 years

The rule is accurate to within a few months for rates between 4% and 12%. It also works backwards: if an investment doubled in 8 years, it earned about 9% a year.

## Compounding frequency

Interest can be added yearly, quarterly, monthly or daily. More frequent compounding helps, but less than people expect. $10,000 at 6% for 20 years:

- Yearly: $32,071
- Quarterly: $32,907
- Monthly: $33,102
- Daily: $33,198

Monthly versus daily is worth about $96 over two decades. The rate and the time matter far more than the frequency. Savings accounts advertise APY (annual percentage yield), which already includes the effect of compounding, so two accounts with the same APY pay the same regardless of how often they compound.

## Regular contributions

Most people do not invest a single lump sum; they add money every month. Compounding then works on each contribution for however long it has left.

$300 a month at 7% for 30 years grows to about $366,000. You contribute $108,000; growth supplies $258,000. The first $300 you put in grows to roughly $2,400 by the end. The last $300 grows to $301.75.

Doubling the contribution doubles the result. Doubling the time more than quadruples it. $300 a month for 15 years reaches about $95,000; for 30 years, $366,000.

## Nominal versus real

A balance of $163,115 in 40 years sounds impressive until you ask what it buys. If prices rise 3% a year, that sum has the purchasing power of about $50,000 today. Your real return was 7% − 3% = roughly 4%, and your real wealth grew about fivefold rather than sixteenfold.

Always think in real terms when planning decades ahead. A simple method is to subtract expected inflation from the expected return and run the numbers at the lower rate. The result is already in today's dollars.

## Compounding against you

Debt compounds too. A credit card balance at 24% APR doubles in about three years if nothing is paid. The month-one interest on $5,000 is $100; pay $125 and only $25 reduces the balance. This is why paying off high-rate debt is often the best "investment" available: eliminating a 24% charge is the same as earning a guaranteed 24% return.

## Fees

Investment fees compound in reverse. A fund charging 1% a year on a portfolio earning 7% leaves you with 6%. Over 40 years, $10,000 grows to $163,000 at 7% but only $109,000 at 6%. The one percentage point of fees consumed a third of the final balance. Low-cost index funds charging 0.05% to 0.2% exist precisely because this effect is so large.

## The practical lessons

1. Start as early as possible; time is the input you cannot buy later.
2. Rate matters more than compounding frequency. Compare APYs, not how often interest is credited.
3. Think in real returns for long horizons.
4. Pay off compounding debt before chasing compounding growth.
5. Keep fees low; they compound just as relentlessly as returns.
