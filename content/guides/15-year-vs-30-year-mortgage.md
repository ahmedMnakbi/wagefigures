The choice between a 15-year and a 30-year mortgage comes down to one trade. The shorter loan costs far less interest in total, but it demands a much larger payment every month. The longer loan keeps the payment low and leaves room in the budget, at the price of paying interest for twice as long.

Neither is right for everyone. The useful thing is to see the actual numbers, so the trade is concrete instead of abstract.

## The same loan, two terms

Take a $300,000 mortgage. Lenders usually price 15-year loans a little lower than 30-year loans, so the table shows the 15-year loan at both the same rate and a rate half a point lower. The rates are illustrative, not quotes.

| Loan | Monthly payment | Total paid | Total interest | Balance after 10 years |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 30 years at 6.0% | $1,798.65 | $647,515 | $347,515 | $251,057 |
| 15 years at 6.0% | $2,531.57 | $455,683 | $155,683 | $130,947 |
| 15 years at 5.5% | $2,451.25 | $441,225 | $141,225 | $128,330 |

Comparing the 30-year loan at 6.0% with the 15-year loan at 5.5%:

- The 15-year payment is $652.60 higher each month.
- Total interest drops from $347,515 to $141,225, a difference of $206,290.
- After 10 years, the 15-year borrower owes about $123,000 less.

Those are the headline numbers. Everything else is about whether the higher payment is a good use of that $652.60.

## Why the shorter loan saves so much

Interest is charged on the outstanding balance every month. A 15-year loan pays the balance down roughly twice as fast, so there is simply less money owed for the interest to be charged on, and for half as many years.

The lower rate helps, but most of the saving comes from time. Even at the same 6.0% rate, the 15-year loan costs $155,683 in interest against $347,515 for the 30-year loan. That is a saving of $191,832 with no rate difference at all.

The [amortization guide](/guides/how-loan-amortization-works/) explains why the early years of a long loan are so interest-heavy. In the first 15 years of the 30-year loan above, the borrower pays $323,757 in total, of which $236,904 is interest, and still owes $213,147.

## What the 30-year loan buys you

The lower payment is not wasted money. It buys flexibility, and that has real value.

**A smaller required payment.** If income falls, a job ends or another expense arrives, the obligation is $1,798.65 rather than $2,451.25. Missing a mortgage payment is expensive, so a payment that fits comfortably in a bad month matters.

**The option to pay it off early anyway.** Most mortgages allow extra principal payments. A borrower on the 30-year loan at 6.0% who voluntarily pays $2,451.25 every month clears the loan in 190 months, about 15 years and 10 months, with $165,219 in total interest. That is only about $24,000 more interest than the true 15-year loan at 5.5%, and they can drop back to the minimum whenever they need to.

**Easier qualification.** Lenders compare the payment to income. A lower payment can mean qualifying for the same loan with less income, or keeping other debt ratios in range.

The gap between $165,219 and $141,225 is essentially the cost of that flexibility: the higher rate on the 30-year loan. Whether it is worth paying depends on how stable the budget is.

## The invest-the-difference argument

A common case for the 30-year loan is to take the lower payment and invest the $652.60 difference each month. Here is how that plays out after 15 years, the point where the 15-year borrower owns the home outright.

The 30-year borrower still owes $213,147. Their investment account depends on the return earned:

| Annual return on investments | Value after 15 years | Minus remaining mortgage | Net position vs. 15-year borrower |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4% | $160,598 | $213,147 | $52,549 behind |
| 6% | $189,788 | $213,147 | $23,359 behind |
| 8% | $225,824 | $213,147 | $12,677 ahead |

In this example, the investing approach only comes out ahead if the investments return more than about 7.4% a year. That is well above the 6.0% mortgage rate, because the 30-year borrower is paying a higher rate than the 15-year borrower and needs the extra return to cover it.

Three things this simple comparison leaves out:

- **Investment returns are not guaranteed.** The mortgage saving is certain. A 7.4% average return over 15 years is possible but can easily fall short.
- **Taxes.** Investment gains may be taxed, and mortgage interest may or may not be deductible depending on individual circumstances. Both shift the break-even point.
- **Discipline.** The comparison only works if the $652.60 is actually invested every month for 15 years. If part of it gets spent, the 15-year loan wins easily.

You can test other returns and amounts with the [monthly investing calculator](/monthly-investing/).

## A quick way to decide

These questions cover most of the decision:

1. **Can you afford the 15-year payment with room to spare?** If the higher payment would leave little emergency savings or force other goals to stop, the 30-year loan is the safer structure.
2. **Is your income steady?** Variable or commission income argues for the lower required payment.
3. **Do you have higher-rate debt?** Paying off a credit card at 20% does more than paying a mortgage early. The [debt payoff calculator](/debt-payoff/) shows how fast that debt can go.
4. **Are retirement contributions on track?** Skipping an employer match to pay a mortgage faster usually gives up a guaranteed return.
5. **Will you really prepay?** If yes, the 30-year loan with extra payments captures most of the saving while keeping flexibility. If not, the 15-year loan enforces it.

## Running your own numbers

Plug your own loan amount and rates into the [mortgage payment calculator](/mortgage-payment/) for both terms. Look at three figures side by side: the monthly payment, the total interest, and the balance after five or ten years. Then ask whether the payment difference fits your budget in a bad month, not just a good one.

The 15-year mortgage is the cheapest way to borrow for a home. The 30-year mortgage is the most forgiving. Seeing the exact dollar gap between the two is what turns the choice from a guess into a decision.
