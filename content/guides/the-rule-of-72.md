The Rule of 72 is a mental shortcut for compound growth. Divide 72 by an annual growth rate, and the answer is roughly how many years it takes for an amount to double. No calculator, no spreadsheet, just one division you can do in your head.

It works for anything that grows at a steady percentage: an investment, a savings account, or prices under inflation. The answer is an estimate, not an exact figure, but it is close enough to be useful and quick enough to use in conversation.

## The formula

Years to double is about 72 divided by the annual percentage rate:

Years to double ≈ 72 / rate

At 6% a year, money doubles in about 72 / 6 = 12 years. At 9%, about 72 / 9 = 8 years. At 4%, about 72 / 4 = 18 years.

You can also run it backwards. If you want to know what rate doubles your money in a set number of years, divide 72 by the years instead. To double in 10 years, you need about 72 / 10 = 7.2% a year.

## How accurate is it?

The true doubling time comes from compound interest math, but the Rule of 72 tracks it closely across the range of rates most people deal with. The table compares the shortcut with the exact figure, assuming the balance compounds once a year.

| Annual rate | Rule of 72 estimate | Exact doubling time | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2% | 36.0 years | 35.0 years | 1.0 year |
| 3% | 24.0 years | 23.5 years | 0.5 year |
| 4% | 18.0 years | 17.7 years | 0.3 year |
| 6% | 12.0 years | 11.9 years | 0.1 year |
| 8% | 9.0 years | 9.0 years | 0.0 year |
| 10% | 7.2 years | 7.3 years | 0.1 year |
| 12% | 6.0 years | 6.1 years | 0.1 year |

The rule is most accurate near 8%, where it is almost exact. It drifts a little at the extremes, overstating the time at very low rates and understating it at very high rates, but the error stays under a year across this whole band. For quick estimates, that is fine.

Some people use 70 or 69 for low rates, since those are closer to the true value there. The reason 72 is the popular choice is that it divides cleanly by 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9 and 12, which covers most of the rates you would ever plug in.

## A worked example

Suppose you invest $10,000 and it grows at 8% a year. The rule says it doubles in about nine years, so you would expect roughly $20,000 after nine years.

Running the exact compound growth gives $19,990 after nine years, within a few dollars of the estimate.

The real power shows up when you let it keep doubling. Each doubling period multiplies the balance by two:

- After 9 years: about $20,000
- After 18 years: about $40,000
- After 27 years: about $80,000
- After 36 years: about $160,000

The $10,000 grows sixteenfold over 36 years without a single extra deposit. That is the engine behind long-term investing, and the Rule of 72 lets you sketch it out in seconds. You can confirm the exact figures with the [compound interest calculator](/compound-interest/).

## Why doubling periods matter more than they seem

The gap between a 6% return and an 8% return looks small on paper. Seen through the Rule of 72, it is the difference between doubling every 12 years and doubling every 9 years.

Over a 36-year career, 6% gives you three doublings. The same money at 8% gives you four. Three doublings multiply a balance by 8. Four doublings multiply it by 16. The higher rate ends with twice as much, from a difference of just two percentage points, because it fits in one extra doubling.

This is why fees matter so much. A 2% annual fee does not just shave 2% off your result. It stretches every doubling period, and over decades that compounds into a large share of the final balance.

## Using it on inflation

The rule works on anything growing at a steady rate, including the cost of living. At 3% inflation, prices double in about 72 / 3 = 24 years. The exact figure is 23.5 years, so the shortcut is close.

That reframes a long-term savings goal. If you are saving for something 24 years away and inflation runs near 3%, it will cost roughly twice as much by the time you get there. A return that merely keeps pace with inflation leaves your buying power flat. To get ahead, your money has to double faster than prices do.

## Related shortcuts

The same idea extends to other multiples by swapping the number on top:

- **Rule of 114** estimates how long money takes to triple. At 8%, about 114 / 8 = 14.3 years, against an exact 14.3 years.
- **Rule of 144** estimates how long money takes to quadruple. At 8%, about 144 / 8 = 18 years, which matches the exact figure almost perfectly, since quadrupling is simply two doublings.

These are handy for the same reason: a single division, no compounding math, and an answer accurate enough to reason with.

## When to reach for it

The Rule of 72 is an estimating tool, not a precision instrument. Use it to:

- Judge whether a rate of return is worth chasing. A jump from 7% to 9% cuts the doubling time from about 10 years to 8.
- Sanity-check a projection before trusting it. If a plan claims to double your money in five years, that implies roughly a 14% annual return, which is high and worth questioning.
- Picture the long-run effect of inflation or fees without opening a spreadsheet.

For an actual plan, where exact dollars and regular contributions matter, use real compound interest math. The Rule of 72 earns its place because it is fast. It turns a vague sense that compounding is powerful into a concrete number you can say out loud, and that is often enough to make a better decision on the spot.

Figures here assume a steady annual rate and ignore taxes. Real returns vary year to year, so treat every estimate as a rough guide rather than a promise.
